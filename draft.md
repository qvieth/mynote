
Governing the Precarious Delta: Adaptation Objects, Intermediaries, and Unequal Outcomes in Vietnam's Mekong’s Rice Bowl
Ha‑Quoc‑Viet Huynh
1. Introduction

The Mekong Delta produces over half of Vietnam's rice and 90 percent of its exports, sustaining more than 15 million smallholder farmers (Nhung & Wang, 2022). Yet this agricultural heartland is increasingly unlivable. The 2015–2016 El Niño–driven drought and saltwater intrusion, the most severe in sixty years, penetrated up to ninety kilometers inland, destroyed thousands of hectares of winter–spring rice, and pushed smallholder households into debt (UNDP, 2016; VietnamNet, 2016). In response, a diverse set of adaptation interventions has emerged: from salinity-control dams and dikes to salt-tolerant rice varieties, parametric insurance schemes, and early-warning monitoring systems (CCAFS, 2016; Nguyen et al., 2024; UNDP, n.d.). Yet such interventions are not neutral technical fixes: they embody particular framings of climate risk, privilege certain forms of expertise, and redistribute costs and benefits across social groups (Vulturius et al., 2017; Woillez & Espagne, 2022), raising urgent questions about whose futures are being secured and whose livelihoods further exposed (Paik et al., 2020).

To investigate how these interventions govern, are produced, and are experienced across scales, and why they generate such unequal adaptive outcomes, this study asks three sub-questions:
How do different adaptation interventions emerge from particular institutional problem‑framings, which shape future envisions?
What infrastructures, intermediaries, and resources are necessary for each intervention to function?
How do farmers navigate between, combine, or resist the different knowledge systems embedded in these interventions when faced with conflicting environmental signals?

This study addresses these questions by tracing translation chains  through a three‑layer framework: a mMacro lLayer of state policy and governance that sets the rules for which adaptations are incentivised; a mMeso lLayer of knowledge politics and intermediation, where extension agents, researchers, traders, and farmers’ own eroding indigenous knowledge negotiate what counts as usable knowledge; and a mMicro lLayer of the three climate adaptation objects three concrete adaptation objects, salt‑tolerant rice varieties, digital advisory apps, and official planting calendars.


2. Analytical Framework and Key Literature
2.1 Translation chain

Drawing on actor-network theory, the sociology of translation (Callon & Latour, 1981; Latour, 1987), and critical political ecology (Forsyth, 2003), scholars have developed the notion of “translation chains” to trace how intermediary actors actively transform, rather than simply transmit, knowledge as it moves between sites of production and practice (Weger, 2023). Weger demonstrates that adaptation knowledge is reshaped by extension agents, researchers, and development staff who re-align it with institutional targets and funding logics, while MacLean's (2013) "papereality" captures how formal reporting structures displace the improvised realities of farmers' fields, rewarding intermediaries who render local heterogeneity legible as scalable "livelihood models."

Yet this framework leaves the agency of adaptation objects themselves undertheorized. Seeds, apps, and calendars are not passive vehicles for knowledge, they are active participants in translation. Star and Griesemer's (1989) boundary object concept offers a corrective: the same planting calendar means something entirely different to a provincial official and a farmer on an ungated field, because it assumes water control that many farmers simply do not have. Li's (2007) "rendering technical" extends this further, converting complex socio-ecological problems into manageable interventions is itself a political act that eliminates certain knowledges before any intermediary even intervenes. Combining Weger's translation chain analysis with boundary object theory and Li's rendering-technical lens allows this study to trace not only how intermediaries reshape knowledge in transit, but how adaptation objects arrive at the field already carrying hierarchical epistemologies, a prior layer of political work the intermediary-focused literature has yet to fully theorize.

2.2 Macro Layer: State Policy and Governance 
At the macro level, the state is the key actor to manage the climate crisis by promoting a "collective economy" model, pressuring farmers into agricultural cooperatives and contract‑farming arrangements with large agribusinesses. These schemes transfer market and environmental risks onto farmers ,  companies mandate strict compliance but frequently refuse off-spec harvests, leaving farmers in debt ,  while land-use zoning traps them within the "rice-first" framework the environmental crisis is already calling into question (Ho & Shattuck, 2025). Programs like the one-million-hectare low-emission rice project aim to scale up sustainable practices, yet remain embedded within the same incentive structures that have historically privileged rice intensification.

This framing gains historical depth when read alongside David Biggs’s (2010) account of colonial attempts to master the Mekong Delta’s fluid ecology. French engineers cut arrow‑straight canals and erected telegraph lines to bypass an unpredictable “quagmire,” yet their rigid infrastructure triggered devastating floods, acidic soil, and cholera. Local inhabitants, meanwhile, moved with the tides and transformed the same “dead points” that stranded European gunboats into “meeting points” (giáp nước) and floating markets. Biggs’s analysis reveals a persistent pattern: top‑down, standardized technocratic solutions that treat the delta as a problem to be conquered routinely overlook situated, relational knowledges. Contemporary climate adaptations risk repeating this pattern. Biggs (2010) identifies a recurring pattern in which top-down, technocratic solutions systematically displace situated and relational knowledges, a tendency that contemporary climate adaptations seem on the verge of repeating the same thing again.
2.3 Meso Layer: Intermediation
Climate adaptation interventions are often proposed in the office and deployed in the field through a dense network of intermediary actors. At this meso layer, translation chains are populated by extension agents, university researchers, and ODA project staff who convert farmers' situated knowledges into "livelihood models" scalable enough to be funded and inserted into provincial socio-economic plans, producing a "papereality" in which official representations of adaptation success displace the improvised realities of farmers' fields (Weger, 2023). This translational work is shaped by output-based development targets and informal networks, and tends to reward entrepreneurial "model farmers" who can convert environmental risk into profit, reinforcing adaptation as individual wealth generation rather than collective systemic change.

Any adequate translation chain analysis must also attend to indigenous and local knowledge: long-settled farming communities have developed rich observational systems for timing agricultural decisions that formal adaptation interventions routinely bypass. Research in An Giang province documented a rich repertoire of Indigenous knowledge (IK)K‑based forecasting heuristics: reading reed shoots, the nesting height of ants and termites, the color of river water, and the behavior of birds and fish to anticipate floods and adjust planting schedules (Pham & Ngo, 2018). However, IK is losing its predictive reliability at precisely the moment it is most needed. Pham and Ngo (2018) found that nearly 90% of respondents in An Giang believed that floods were "no longer predictable" using traditional indicators. Specific folk markers, the "Year of the Dragon brings big floods," the correlation between bamboo shoot height and water level, were cited as increasingly unreliable. The 2015 flood, historically small, defied multiple traditional signs that had pointed toward a larger event (Pham & Ngo, 2018). This erosion is not a failure of IK by itself but a consequence of the fundamental transformation of the delta's hydrology.
2.4 Micro Layer: Climate Adaptation Objects
The macro state policy and knowledge are materialized in the Vietnamese Mekong Delta into three climate adaptation objects: salt‑tolerant rice varieties, digital advisory apps, and official planting calendars. Each climate adaptation objects are innovated by different authority at  multiple sites of knowledge production.
 
Salt-tolerant rice varieties are a matter of livelihood survival: salinity intrusion now penetrating ninety kilometres inland threatens the biological foundation of rice production. Three distinct pipelines shape their circulation: a farmer-breeder pipeline (TC7, developed by Hoa Sĩ Hiền through 15 years of improvised saline selection, tolerating 4‰, recognised 2019); a national research pipeline (Professor Lang's OM series and AS996, stalled by regulatory delays, a Tuổi Trẻ investigation found six OM lines trapped in registration while salinity destroyed crops, exposing a temporal mismatch between breeding cycles of 5–10 years and salinity pulses of weeks); and an international-linked pipeline (HATRI 200, tolerating 5‰, yielding 6.5–7 tonnes/ha, but potentially excluding farmers outside certified seed systems). As Weger (2023) predicts, movement from pipeline to field is never direct: varieties pass through extension agents, officials, and traders who filter them through criteria of profitability, legibility, and development targets.

Digital advisory apps promise to manage water and soil from a distance, yet their adaptive capacity is conditioned by who can access them, whose data they capture, and whose decisions they serve. Three programs have emerged: a donor-backed IoT-AWD system (MimosaTEK) delivering water savings but requiring flat terrain, electricity, and smartphone ownership; a commercial smart-irrigation platform enabling remote salinity monitoring but creating dependencies on group investment models; and the state-linked FarMoRe/RiceMoRe app embedding farmers within a hierarchical data-reporting system where they become data suppliers with limited control over their own information.

Official planting calendars represent the state's temporal-coordinative response, translating hydro-meteorological science into standardised sowing windows, yet their protective capacity proves inseparable from the hard infrastructure dividing gated fields from ungated margins. After the 2015–2016 crisis, the state intensified "Lịch Thời Vụ" windows to help farmers "né mặn" (avoid salt). However, Le et al. (2025) demonstrate that compliance yields significant benefits only for gated fields; in ungated areas, farmers are more likely to comply yet experience lower yields and profits. This mirrors the state's "rice-first" zoning: both presume a controlled, gated landscape that many farmers do not inhabit. When the calendar fails outside the gates, it is not a technical error but a translation gap, a "papereality" that cannot accommodate the unmediated hydrology of ungated fields.

3. Methodology
This study employs a qualitative, multi-site design across Trà Vinh, Sóc Trăng, and An Giang, each selected for its distinct salinity exposure and adaptation infrastructure. Trà Vinh hosts an active World Bank–Korea IoT-AWD pilot; Sóc Trăng contains both gated and ungated fields enabling direct infrastructural comparison; An Giang is home to farmer-breeder Hoa Sĩ Hiền and to communities where IK erosion is documented. Fieldwork over approximately three months examines three to four bounded "intervention episodes" to ensure all three analytical layers are studied together in practice.

Participants across 15–30 individuals span three groups: smallholder farmers (sampled for variation by location, cooperative membership, and smartphone access); intermediaries (extension workers, seed traders, cooperative managers, app support staff); and designers and institutional actors (breeders, developers, calendar officials, low-emission project staff). Semi-structured interviews are supplemented by participant observation during critical decision periods. A timeline-drawing exercise asks farmers to plot key decisions against official sowing windows, salinity pulses, and app-update moments, directly capturing temporal mismatches that interviews alone might miss.

4. Research Objectives
Building on the three sub-questions, the study’s overarching objective is to examine how climate adaptation interventions in Vietnam’s Mekong Delta are framed, translated, materialized, and experienced across macro-, meso-, and micro-levels, and why they produce unequal adaptive outcomes. The first objective is to identify and compare the institutional problem-framings and future visions through which salt-tolerant rice varieties, digital advisory apps, and official planting calendars are produced and promoted at the macro level. The second objective is to map the infrastructures, intermediaries, resources, and translation practices through which each adaptation object reaches farmers in Trà Vinh, Sóc Trăng, and An Giang, including how extension agents, researchers, traders, and cooperative managers reshape knowledge in transit. The third objective is to analyze how farmers navigate, combine, or resist the knowledge systems embedded in these interventions when faced with conflicting environmental signals, with particular attention to differences between gated and ungated fields. The fourth objective is to synthesize findings into actionable recommendations for Vietnam’s one-million-hectare low-emission rice project and digital agriculture policies, identifying where adaptation tools fail the most vulnerable farmers and what institutional adjustments could make them more equitable. These objectives operationalize the three sub-questions while adding an explicit policy-synthesis objective consistent with the study’s stated contributions.

5. Fieldwork Schedule and Timeline
The fieldwork will be organized over an indicative twelve-week period, with flexibility to align observation and interviews with critical decision periods such as salinity pulses, official sowing windows, and app-update moments. The schedule distributes the three to four bounded intervention episodes across the three sites. During Weeks 1 and 2, based in HCMC, Cần Thơ, and provincial offices, the research will obtain ethics and provincial permissions, finalize access, recruit participants, pilot-test interview guides and the timeline-drawing exercise, and check translation and consent materials. The main output of this phase will be final instruments, a participant map, and a pilot memo.

During Weeks 3 to 5, fieldwork in Trà Vinh will examine the World Bank–Korea IoT-AWD pilot as an intervention episode. This phase will involve interviews with five to ten farmers, intermediaries, and designers, observation during water and irrigation decision moments, and the timeline-drawing exercise. The main outputs will be a Trà Vinh site memo, transcripts, and observation logs. During Weeks 6 to 8, fieldwork in Sóc Trăng will compare gated and ungated fields through interviews with five to ten participants, observation of calendar compliance and salinity-monitoring practices, and documentation of one or two intervention episodes. The main outputs will be a comparative site memo and gated/ungated observation notes.

During Weeks 9 and 10, fieldwork in An Giang will trace the farmer-breeder and seed pipeline through interviews with five to ten farmers, seed traders, extension staff, and breeders, and will document indigenous knowledge erosion and forecasting practices. The main outputs will be an An Giang site memo, transcripts, and seed-pipeline notes. During Week 11, across sites or remotely, the research will conduct follow-up interviews, a stakeholder debrief, and member-checking of preliminary interpretations, and will clean and organize data. The main outputs will be validation notes and a cleaned data set. During Week 12, based at a desk or in a provincial capital, the research will complete transcription checks, begin coding, prepare preliminary findings, and write a fieldwork report. The main outputs will be a fieldwork report and a preliminary analytical matrix. If the grant period is shorter than twelve weeks, Weeks 11 and 12 can be compressed into a ten-week plan by conducting follow-ups remotely and completing the fieldwork report after return.

6. Fieldwork Budget Plan

The multi-site research design across An Giang, Sóc Trăng, and Trà Vinh requires a total estimated budget of NT$39,500 for the three-month fieldwork period (calculated at the October 2026 exchange rate of approximately NT$1 = 793 VND). In accordance with the Global Asia Research Center (GARC) funding guidelines, this application requests NT$30,000 from GARC to subsidize international airfare, inter-provincial transportation, rural motorbike rental, and part of field accommodation. The remaining NT$9,500 will be self-funded by the applicant to cover fuel and local ride-hailing, the balance of accommodation, fieldwork logistics and participatory materials, and informant hospitality tokens.

International and Inter-Provincial Transportation (NT$12,300; GARC-funded)

International travel requires a round-trip economy class ticket between Taipei (TPE) and Ho Chi Minh City (SGN). Based on current 2026 airfare benchmarks—ranging from NT$7,500 on low-cost carriers (including checked baggage for research materials) to NT$9,350 on full-service airlines such as China Airlines or EVA Air—international airfare is budgeted at NT$8,500 × 1 = NT$8,500, funded by GARC. Upon arrival in Ho Chi Minh City, inter-provincial transit between the urban hub, the regional research base in Cần Thơ, and the three provincial field sites (An Giang, Sóc Trăng, and Trà Vinh) will be conducted via long-distance sleeper and limousine buses (such as FUTA Bus Lines/Phương Trang and regional operators). At standard 2026 fares of 180,000 to 300,000 VND (approx. NT$230 to NT$380) per leg, the budget uses NT$380 per leg × 10 legs = NT$3,800, funded by GARC. The transportation subtotal is NT$12,300.

Rural Field Mobility and Local Transit (NT$7,200)

Investigating how climate adaptation objects operate across both sluice-gated interiors and remote, ungated margins requires independent daily transport along rural canal embankments and commune roads not served by public transit. A monthly manual motorbike rental in the Mekong Delta costs approximately 1,500,000 VND (NT$1,900) per month, budgeted as NT$1,900 × 3 months = NT$5,700, funded by GARC. Fuel and short-distance ride-hailing are budgeted at NT$500 per month × 3 months = NT$1,500, self-funded, based on current Vietnamese retail gasoline prices (approx. 20,500 VND or NT$26 per liter) plus occasional GrabBike/taxi use in provincial centers. The rural mobility subtotal is NT$7,200.

Field Site Accommodation (NT$16,500)

While base stays will utilize economical student and family housing networks, conducting multi-day ethnographic immersion and consecutive interview blocks across An Giang, Sóc Trăng, and Trà Vinh requires approximately 30 nights of paid lodging in district-level guesthouses (nhà nghỉ) and standard local hotels near agricultural cooperatives. At the standard provincial rate of 400,000 to 450,000 VND (approx. NT$550) per night, field accommodation is budgeted as NT$550 × 30 nights = NT$16,500. Of this amount, NT$12,000 is requested from GARC and NT$4,500 is self-funded by the applicant.

Fieldwork Logistics and Participatory Materials (NT$3,500; Self-funded)

Conducting 15 to 30 semi-structured interviews and participatory timeline-drawing exercises requires localized material support. Participatory timeline printing, stationery, and a local 4G/5G data plan are budgeted at NT$500 × 3 = NT$1,500, self-funded. This covers large-format seasonal calendar templates, hydro-meteorological charts, stationery for farmer timeline-drawing exercises, and a Viettel/Vinaphone SIM/data plan for field coordination and cloud backup of audio recordings. In keeping with rural Vietnamese fieldwork norms when visiting smallholder households and agricultural cooperatives, NT$80 × 25 = NT$2,000 is allocated for modest, culturally appropriate hospitality tokens (such as tea, fruit, or refreshments during 60–90-minute interview sessions), self-funded. The logistics and materials subtotal is NT$3,500.

7. Expected Outcomes
This study brings adaptation interventions directly into conversation with the social and political processes that determine whether it strengthens or undermines adaptation. By tracing how the same adaptation objects function differently inside and outside salinity gates, how intermediaries gatekeep knowledge through translation chains, and how farmers navigate between eroding IK and rigid state calendars, the study exposes the hidden governance, knowledge-politics, and infrastructural conditions that make climate services effective for some and maladaptive for others. Theoretically, it advances Science and Technology Studies by extending boundary object theory beyond institutional negotiation to show how adaptation objects arrive at the field already pre-loaded with hierarchical epistemologies, a prior layer of political work that intermediary-focused translation chain accounts have left undertheorized; it deepens Political Ecology by operationalizing Li's (2007) "rendering technical" at the object level, demonstrating how seeds, apps, and calendars silently eliminate certain knowledges before any intermediary intervenes; and it contributes to Vietnamese Studies by historicizing these dynamics within Biggs's (2010) account of technocratic delta-making, showing that the contemporary push for digital agriculture and low-emission rice production reproduces, rather than breaks from, a centuries-long pattern of standardized, infrastructure-dependent solutions that systematically marginalize farmers inhabiting the ungated, fluid margins of the delta. Its findings will directly inform Vietnam's one-million-hectare low-emission rice project and the broader push for digital agriculture or different climate adaptation plans, identifying where tools fail the most vulnerable, and what institutional adjustments could make them genuinely equitable.
